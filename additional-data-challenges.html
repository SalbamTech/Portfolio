Responsible Machine Learning: Data Challenge Scenarios

Three practical cases showing how data quality, time, imbalance, fairness, and explanation requirements change the way a machine-learning system should be designed and evaluated.

Description

From data problems to defensible decisions.

The original activity used an AI coach to present progressively complex data scenarios. Each one required a decision, supporting reasoning, and a response to feedback. I adapted that work into a professional case study focused on the assumptions, trade-offs, and safeguards that generalize to real ML projects.

Tangible artifact

Three scenarios, three risk profiles

Scenario 01

Missing telecom data

Challenge: Fifteen percent of customer-service call counts were missing or incomplete in a churn dataset.

Decision: Investigate the missingness mechanism, reconcile records with source logs, add a missingness indicator, and compare robust imputation methods.

Control: Fit preprocessing on training data only, run sensitivity tests, and monitor missingness by time, plan, and call center.

Scenario 02

Fraud under temporal shift

Challenge: Fraud was rare, increased sharply, changed form, and included a feature collected only in recent months.

Decision: Use time-based validation, class weighting, operational metrics, and separate stable-feature and recent-feature models.

Control: Test on the newest mature labels, retain a fallback model, and monitor drift after deployment.

Scenario 03

Biased hiring text

Challenge: Resume text was unstructured, historical labels were noisy, outcomes differed by group, and explanations were required.

Decision: Use decision support only, extract job-related evidence, create rubric-based labels, and favor an interpretable approach.

Control: Evaluate subgroup errors, run matched-resume tests, preserve human review, and provide an appeal path.

Reusable method

A five-step decision framework

  1. DiagnoseIdentify how the data problem arose before selecting a statistical fix.
  2. ProtectPrevent leakage and preserve realistic time, prevalence, and deployment conditions.
  3. CompareTest reasonable alternatives instead of relying on one preferred technique.
  4. EvaluateUse metrics tied to business costs, affected groups, and operational capacity.
  5. MonitorDefine drift, fairness, and data-quality signals that trigger investigation or retraining.
Central lessonA model can be technically accurate and still be unreliable or harmful if its data assumptions, validation design, explanations, or deployment controls are weak.
